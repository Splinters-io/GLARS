Roadmap

From a snapshot to a moving picture.

What changes the risk after the score is set?

GLARS scores each jurisdiction against the same criteria. That is a sound baseline. This roadmap sets out five enhancements that would move it from static, country-level scores to assessments that reflect time, context and the way jurisdictions interact.

Where it stops
today

Five things a static score can't see.

The current method captures the primary jurisdictional risks. These are its limits.

Time

Static assessment

Country-level scores stay fixed while legal landscapes and geopolitical situations change quickly.

Context

Context insensitivity

The same score applies whatever the industry, the data or the organisation, which leaves blind spots.

Capability

Technical gap

The technical capability to enforce laws varies widely between jurisdictions, and the model doesn't fully reflect it.

Combination

Binary logic

Taking the maximum score across jurisdictions misses how overlapping legal regimes interact.

History

Historical blindness

Past behaviour and enforcement patterns aren't systematically included, though they help predict what comes next.

None of these undo the baseline. They show where it can go next.

Enhancement 01
Temporal Intelligence Factors

Laws change. Scores should too.

Legal frameworks evolve, enforcement priorities shift and political climates change. Temporal Intelligence Factors (TIF) add three time-sensitive dimensions.

LV

Legislative velocity

The rate of change in the relevant legal frameworks. Faster change means more compliance uncertainty, and a higher score.

  • New laws and amendments in the past 24 months
  • Pending legislation likely to pass
  • How often regulatory guidance changes
ETV

Enforcement trend vector

The direction of enforcement activity. Rising enforcement raises the score; falling enforcement lowers it.

  • Year-on-year change in enforcement actions
  • Changing penalties and fines
  • Shifts in agency priorities and leadership statements
GS

Geopolitical stability

Political factors that could trigger abrupt legal change. Lower stability raises the overall risk.

  • Likelihood of electoral transition
  • Status of diplomatic relationships
  • Indicators of international tension

Temporal adjustment

TIF Score = Base Score × (1 + (LV × 0.2) + (ETV × 0.3) - (GS × 0.2))

Each country gets a baseline, then trend vectors adjust it as these dimensions change. GLARS becomes a time series rather than a snapshot: rapid legislative change and rising enforcement increase the score, and greater stability reduces it.

Enhancement 02
Industry Context Multipliers

The same country. A different risk for each industry.

A healthcare provider and a gaming company in the same jurisdiction can face very different data access risk, because of the regulations specific to each. Industry Context Multipliers (ICM) give each industry its own profile.

IndustryMultiplier rangePrimary factors
Healthcare1.2 – 1.5Patient privacy regulations, research data protections
Financial services1.3 – 1.6Banking secrecy laws, financial intelligence access
Critical infrastructure1.4 – 1.7National security provisions, emergency powers
Telecommunications1.3 – 1.5Lawful intercept requirements, metadata retention
Education1.1 – 1.3Student privacy protections, research exceptions
Consumer technology0.9 – 1.2Consumer protection focus, commercial privacy laws

Industry adjustment

Industry-Adjusted Score = Base Score × ICM

Each multiplier applies to specific GLARS components rather than the overall score. Healthcare might face higher Agency Powers (AP) scores but lower Technical Requirements (TR) scores in some jurisdictions. Technical Requirements include powers like the UK's technical capability notices, which can compel providers to modify their systems for surveillance. The multiplier comes from a matrix of industry-specific regulatory factors for each jurisdiction.

Enhancement 03
Enforcement History Factors

What the law says, and what actually happens.

Written law often differs from how it is applied. Enforcement History Factors (EHF) use documented enforcement actions to check theoretical legal risk against practice. Each factor is scored from 0 to 100.

FEF

Foreign entity focus

Whether a jurisdiction disproportionately targets foreign entities in enforcement actions.

ETE

Extraterritorial enforcement

The jurisdiction's history of enforcing its laws beyond its borders.

MPA

Max penalty application

How often the maximum available penalties are imposed, rather than more moderate sanctions.

IA

Investigative aggressiveness

How often and how deeply the government investigates, including investigations that end without formal action.

ASR

Appeal success rate

How often enforcement actions are successfully challenged on appeal, as a measure of oversight.

Enforcement-adjusted components

APadjusted = APbase × (1 + ((FEF + ETE + IA) ÷ 300))

JOadjusted = JObase × (1 - (ASR ÷ 100))

EXadjusted = EXbase × (1 + (ETE ÷ 100))

The factors adjust specific components rather than the overall score, so the adjustment follows actual behaviour rather than theoretical powers. It needs a maintained database of enforcement actions across jurisdictions, categorised by industry, entity type and outcome. Machine learning could then find patterns and predict enforcement priorities.

Enhancement 04
Data Sensitivity Factors

Not all data carries the same risk.

Government access to marketing copy and government access to biometric data are not the same event. Data Sensitivity Factors (DSF) adjust the score by data classification.

Critical 2.0–2.5×

  • National security information
  • Biometric identification data
  • Financial authentication credentials
  • Nuclear and defence technical data

Sensitive 1.5–2.0×

  • Personal health information
  • Financial transaction records
  • Political affiliation data
  • Genetic information

Confidential 1.2–1.5×

  • Business trade secrets
  • Non-public personal data
  • Intellectual property
  • Infrastructure details

Restricted 1.0–1.2×

  • Customer contact information
  • Employee HR records
  • Internal business communications
  • Product development information

Public 0.8–1.0×

  • Published materials
  • Open data sets
  • Product marketing information
  • Public statements and disclosures

Sensitivity adjustment

DSF-Adjusted Score = Base Score × DSF Multiplier

The organisation states the classification during the assessment, or the highest applicable level is used by default. Jurisdictions may classify data types differently, which adds a matrix of jurisdiction-specific sensitivity factors.

Enhancement 05
Jurisdiction Interaction Model

Jurisdictions amplify each other, or hold each other back.

Data flows often cross several overlapping jurisdictions. Taking the highest score oversimplifies them. The Jurisdiction Interaction Model (JIM) replaces that with an analysis of how the jurisdictions interact.

Cooperative enforcement Amplifying

Mutual legal assistance treaties and cooperation agreements can give jurisdictions a combined reach greater than either alone.

Example: Five Eyes intelligence sharing, especially where a power like the UK's technical capability notices can be used across partner nations. See it in the worked example.

Legal conflict Mitigating

When legal requirements directly conflict, the impossibility of complying with both can protect the entity.

Example: GDPR Article 48 limits the effect of foreign legal demands, which may reduce the effectiveness of the US CLOUD Act.

Blocking statutes Mitigating

Laws written to block the extraterritorial reach of another jurisdiction's laws can reduce the effective risk.

Example: the EU Blocking Statute prohibits compliance with certain US sanctions against Iran.

Corporate structure Amplifying

Parent-subsidiary relationships and operating structures create exposure that data location alone doesn't show.

Example: a US parent's control over an EU subsidiary's data creates US legal exposure despite EU data localisation.

Data localisation Variable

A requirement to keep data in a jurisdiction can raise local access risk while lowering foreign access risk.

Example: Russian data localisation law increases FSB access while potentially reducing US intelligence access.

Interaction adjustment

Interaction Adjustment = (JA × JB × Relationship Factor) - Blocking Effect

where JA and JB are the jurisdiction risk scores

The relationship factor amplifies risk for cooperating regimes; the blocking effect reduces it where legal conflicts make compliance impossible. Building this means mapping the legal relationships between jurisdictions, possibly as a graph with weighted edges for each type of relationship. Technical capability powers like the UK's technical capability notices should weigh heavily, because they can change the security of systems across jurisdictions.

Getting there

Four steps from roadmap to tool.

The baseline method is useful now. These steps would add the enhancements to it.

Step 01

Extend the data

  1. Track legislative changes and enforcement actions over time
  2. Build industry classification matrices for major jurisdictions
  3. Build a repository of historical enforcement actions
  4. Define a data sensitivity classification
  5. Map the relationships between jurisdictions
Step 02

Update the calculation

  1. Apply temporal adjustment factors
  2. Add industry-specific modifiers
  3. Feed enforcement history into component scores
  4. Apply data sensitivity multipliers
  5. Implement the jurisdiction interaction model
Step 03

Validate and calibrate

  1. Compare against known enforcement patterns and case studies
  2. Test how sensitive results are to each weighting
  3. Look back at past cross-border legal conflicts
  4. Check predictions with panels of legal experts
  5. Adjust weights and factors from what validation shows
Step 04

Make it usable

  1. Let users choose which enhancements to apply
  2. Visualise complex interactions
  3. Take data sensitivity and industry as inputs
  4. Compare scenarios with different enhancements
  5. Document every part of the method
The direction

From a risk score to risk intelligence.

Together, these enhancements take GLARS beyond a single score. It would account for time, context, history, sensitivity and interaction, and give a more accurate basis for data governance decisions across jurisdictions.

Regulation keeps changing and data flows keep getting more complex. The need for this kind of assessment will only grow.
